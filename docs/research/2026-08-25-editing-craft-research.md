# The craft of editorial selection

How professional photographers, picture editors and film editors take a large body of material down to a final selection. Sources named inline. Quotes are verbatim from the cited source; paraphrases and my own inferences are labelled. Where I could not corroborate something, it says so.

---

## 1. Pass structure

### 1a. Photographers: the shoot-to-delivery ladder

The best-documented structure is David Hurn's (Magnum), written down in *On Being a Photographer* (Hurn & Bill Jay, LensWork, 3rd ed. 2001), because he published his actual grease-pencil colour code. It is three separate decisions taken over the same contact sheets at three different times:

- **Pass 1 — "anything of interest", white.** Immediate: "As soon as I see the contacts, I mark any image of interest, for whatever reason, in white." The question is *is there a picture here at all*, not *is it good*.
- **The wait.** "Then, if at all possible, I like to put aside the contacts for a few weeks. This serves to distance me from the emotion of the picture-taking moment so that I am better able to see the image dispassionately."
- **Pass 2 — "useful for this project", yellow.** The question shifts from quality to fitness for a purpose.
- **Pass 3 — "final selections", red.** What gets published, exhibited or sold. Again made "at a later date".

He keeps a fourth, non-editorial colour (blue) for prints promised to subjects — the marking system carries obligations, not just quality judgements. The reason for the enforced gap, in his words: "Too often when we look at our own pictures we remember the excitement of the event, which becomes mixed up with our cool judgment of the results. Then again, if an image was particularly difficult to shoot, we justify it: something so hard to achieve must be worthwhile." Gregory Halpern (Magnum) reports the same mechanism in Magnum's *Theory & Practice* piece on editing and sequencing: "Sometimes I'll shoot film and I don't get it developed for a month," because a fresh reaction is contaminated by the memory of shooting. Survival rate, per Hurn: "Presumably, when a photographer presses the button it is because he believes the image is worthwhile. It rarely is."

**Several fast passes beat one careful pass** — unanimous across the teachers, for three separate reasons. Attention depletes (duChemin: "limited resources of time and attention, which wane after a few thousand images"). Load per frame drops once the floor is cleared (Alex Cooke: "Two fast passes will almost always outperform one agonizing marathon"). And the later pass genuinely sees differently — duChemin: "I almost always find images on subsequent edits that I'd have missed on the first even if I paid much more attention"; Raymond Depardon: "as time goes by, pictures become transformed. They gain in value." Ming Thein turns this into an algorithm — **keep the intersection of two independent passes**: "I make one set of selections close to the time of capture… then a second set perhaps a few days later; **those that overlap are the ones that make it into the final cut**." He names the bias the delay defeats, effort bias: "you're more likely to remember an image that took more effort to produce… even if the latter image is a 'better' one" — and the upper bound on waiting: too long and "you may forget what the intended idea was".

Almost nobody deletes the rejects — duChemin: "you're not deleting your un-chosen images… The others are still there and will be waiting when you come back." Thein re-rates rather than deletes; MPW's rule is absolute — "No deletions." Note also that the passes run along different *axes*: duChemin's three are temporal (day of shooting, "a couple weeks or months" later, "a year or so" out); Thein's are by pipeline position ("one almost immediately after shooting, in-camera; one when I get home and dump the cards…; and the final cut… before I archive or deliver").

### 1b. Ratios that are actually documented

- **Missouri Photo Workshop** (since 1949; faculty are Pulitzer / NatGeo / World Press photographers). Its 75-year rules are three lines: "No posed photographs. No more than 400 images. No deletions." Each photographer produces a **10–15 image picture story**, edited with a faculty picture editor **every evening**, not once at the end. ~3% survival, edit distributed across the shoot.
- **Working photographers, self-reported funnels.** Tony Wodarck (wedding): ~5,000 shot → ~600 delivered → 100–150 highlights, and he culls "5,000 images in about 1-1.5 hours" (~1 s/frame) — the cull is the cheap stage; editing the 600 takes ~8 hours. Jared Platt: "I deliver 15% for a small wedding and 10% on a larger wedding", and at situation level "you are shooting 90 photos to give them 3." David duChemin: "I came home from my last trip with almost 20,000 photographs… I edited them down to about 30." Ming Thein: "I force myself to keep only the best 1-2% of everything I shoot." Deadline sport is faster and looser: Joshua Genz culls "~2,000 images in about 15 minutes and will typically tag around 10%"; Scott Kelby tags 10–15 at halftime and cuts to "six or eight at best"; Jim Colton (ex-*Sports Illustrated*) says field photographers "choose what they believe are the 10-15 best, and send those off."
- **Robert Frank, *The Americans*.** SFMOMA's *Looking In* press material gives it exactly: "767 rolls of film", "more than 27,000 images", "more than 1,000 work prints", 83 images in the book, and "He then spent a year editing, selecting, and sequencing the photographs." Photobook Club, on the same scholarship, adds an intermediate rung: he "selected and printed 1000 work prints, which he pinned to the wall of his Third Avenue apartment, or laid on the floor, slowly editing these prints to just 100, and then the 83 that would make up the final sequence." Shape: 27,000 → 1,000 → 100 → 83. The first cut is huge and cheap; the last three rungs are slow and expensive, and the last one barely cuts anything.
- **National Geographic, per story.** Sarah Leen, then Director of Photography, gives the whole funnel in HuffPost: "Let's say it's 12,000 pictures" → "We'll make an edit of about maybe 50 that we'll be using for the 50 top images for the story. In print it's going to be a lot less. It could be only 12, 15, 18." Sadie Quarrier, Deputy Director of Photography, on Nat Geo's *Overheard* podcast: "We would go through maybe 30,000 images on average for a story." Leen also insists the editor sees the raw take, not the photographer's shortlist: "if they shot 10,000 images, I look at 10,000 images and edit" (thecameracafeshow.com).
- **A book edit with numbers.** Magnum's account of Mark Power editing 21 years of archive with Stuart Smith of GOST Books over three days: "the pair began by looking at over 4,000 pictures to get to an edit of 600 prints, from which 196 were finally selected." Two narrowing passes, ~7:1 then ~3:1 — not one pass to the answer.
- **One situation.** Steve Winter, on a Nat Geo assignment, culls 112 frames to about 8 and then to 1 in a filmed Wired session. And *Apocalypse Now*: ~1.25 million feet / ~230 hours of workprint, two years of cutting, four credited picture editors including Murch (cinemontage.org; AFI catalog). The often-quoted 95:1 ratio has no primary source I could verify.

### 1c. Film: assembly → rough → fine → picture lock

Consistent across craft sources (Frame.io's *Stages of the Editing Process*; Noam Kroll's "My Picture Lock Workflow"; Wikipedia's *Rough cut* / *Picture lock*):

- **Selects / circle takes** — per setup: which take is alive.
- **Assembly (first cut)** — every scene in order, one take each, nothing trimmed. Frame.io: "usually quite long, sometimes more than twice the length of the final film", and built "slowly and steadily from the previous day's dailies" *during* the shoot, not after it. Murch's advice for this stage, in his *Art of the Cut* interview with Steve Hullfish (ProVideo Coalition), is to cut "with your eyes half-closed": decide without applying full critical judgement, because the whole is not yet visible.
- **Rough cut** — structure and pacing; Frame.io's guide puts "most of the blood, sweat and tears" here, over months.
- **Fine cut, then picture lock** — the rough cut refines the film, the fine cut refines each cut; at lock, no further editorial change.

### 1d. Documentary: the material comes first, the story second

Closest analogue to a personal archive, because nobody wrote a script. The **Alliance of Documentary Editors**' *Guide for Documentary Edit Schedules* (Nov 2021, compiled from hundreds of working doc editors) is the best primary source on both the passes and their cost:

- **Screening and logging** — "On average editors require **2 hours to screen and take notes on every hour of footage**." Transcripts time-coded before the editor starts.
- **Stringouts** — all usable material for a topic/character/scene laid end to end; ADE expects multi-cam footage to be "grouped and strung out" *before* the editor arrives.
- **Selects** — best moments pulled from the stringouts into per-theme or per-day reels.
- **Paper edit / radio cut** — for interview-driven films the structure is built as text from transcripts, then as a sound-only assembly with no regard for visuals.
- **Assembly → rough → fine → lock.** New Doc Editing's *Stages of Documentary Post-Production* gives convergence targets: build the assembly once "ninety percent of your footage is shot, digitized and logged" and keep it to at most **140% of final length** (84 min for a 60-min film); rough cut within **10%** of final runtime; fine cut within **3%**. Its per-stage questions are worth copying verbatim — at assembly, "Is there a story here?" and "Is there a film here?"; at rough cut, does the structure and rhythm work on an audience; at fine cut, is everything aligned before lock.

**Documented documentary funnels, named.** Murch on *Coup 53*: "ultimately 532 hours, the most I've ever had to deal with" → "I created four timelines — each of them about three or four hours long" → "That produced a single eight-and-a-half-hour timeline" → a 118-minute film. Mo Stoebe on *Kedi*: "about 180 hours of footage… I used that period to create selects reels for each feature cat… assembling rough cuts for each featured feline as well as string-outs of all the interviews. This whole process took about three months", then "After the 33 assemblies resulted in a structure we liked, we spent another month or so until we locked picture" — ten months to 79 minutes. Bob Eisenhardt gives the selects survival rate, and it is **not** aggressive: "In documentary, you get selects that come down to 50 percent — 25 percent in this case, thank God — of all the material." Jake Pushinsky relays Jeff Wolf's heuristic as repeated halving: "You start with 100 hours of footage. Your first goal is to get it down to 50 hours… then your goal is to get it down to twenty five hours." Steve Audette (FRONTLINE) names the shooting ratio — "Our shooting ratio is something like 200:1" — and the convergence: "the rough cut is usually eight minutes too long, the fine cuts might be four minutes long." Murch's rule for a first cut is "~30% long", and "If it's more than 30 percent longer, that's a red flag." And the human limit on the screening pass, from Jonathan Oppenheim: "You can screen about five hours a day, six if you really push it, before losing all perspective"; Tom Haneke: "I could only hold 25 hours at a time… out of about 500 hours of raw material."

ADE's headline rule: **one month of editing per ten minutes of finished content** — a 90-minute feature is a nine-month edit; multi-storyline films "often edit for well over a year". Two ADE quotes that name what is happening: Travis Swartz (producer) — "Editing a documentary is akin to someone handing you a bag of sentences and asking you to write a book"; Steph Ching (editor) — "Watching down all the footage at the beginning of the process, without feeling rushed, really matters. You won't just see and understand all the content that you have, but your initial emotional reactions to how things actually play out on camera, in real time, can help reveal the story and are probably indicative of what your audience will feel at different points in your film."

Murch, in *Art of the Cut*, on dailies: "You're only going to see this material for the first time once, and so whatever your initial impressions are, that's the closest you're ever going to get to how the audience is going to feel." Kelley Dixon, ACE (*Breaking Bad*), same series: "Remember how it made me feel the first time you saw it because the audience is going to see it as you saw that first time." Three independent practitioners, one doctrine: **the first viewing is the only unbiased instrument you have, and it is single-use.**

### 1e. What the editor is looking AT, per pass

This is the part most tooling gets wrong, so it is worth isolating (synthesis, drawn from the sources above):

| Pass | Unit under the eye |
| --- | --- |
| Technical cull | the single frame, alone |
| Selects | the frame, against its neighbours in the same burst or setup |
| Grouping / stringout | the situation or scene as a block |
| Assembly / work-print layout | the whole set at once — wall, floor, contact sheet, timeline |
| Fine cut / sequencing | adjacent pairs, and the whole run in one sitting |

Spencer Averick names the failure of judging at the wrong level (*Art of the Cut*): "Sometimes when you're watching dailies in the context of just dailies, it doesn't translate into the scene itself."

Murch's still-frame wall is the physical form of the fourth row, and he states its purpose exactly (*Art of the Cut*): "I have panels of printed images of selected moments, frames, from every camera position in the scene that I am working on", giving "a bird's-eye view of the whole scene in a single glance" and preventing "certain moments from being overlooked or forgotten." The marking question is functional, not evaluative — "what I think might be key frames that answer the question: 'Why did they shoot this shot?'… Is he angry? Find the decisive angry moment. Is she sad? Find the sad moment." Frames print "about twenty-five images on a page". In use: "I look at the wall of images to see what frame jumps out at me as: 'CHOOSE ME!'… It's like a class full of people raising their hand." A whole take is reduced to one representative still, and selection happens *spatially and in parallel*, not by scrubbing. Hurn does the identical thing with contact sheets, and with prints on a wall for a finished essay.

## 2. Binary vs graded

The high-volume answer is **binary, repeated** — not graded once.

- Lightroom's flag model is two-state per pass: P to pick, X to reject.
- Hurn's colour system *looks* graded but is not: white / yellow / red are **three separate binary decisions asked at three different times with three different questions**, not one 1–5 score. This is the structural point.
- Photo Mechanic — what photojournalists and sports shooters use on deadline — is built for tagged/colour-class binary sorting at speed; Camera Bits' own material and the PPA's *Photo Mechanic Power Tips* describe number-key colour classes plus filter widgets, and the design goal is zero lag so the operator "can get into a rhythm".

Named advocates for binary, with their reasons — this is the best-attested finding in the report:

- **David duChemin**: edits "should be selections, not ratings. Pick them or don't pick them, but don't rate them"; "trying to decide whether an image deserves 2, 3, or 4 stars slows the process… rating them makes me look for the wrong thing. **For me, a 3-star image isn't a Yes! It's a yawn.**" He also names the failure mode of graded culling — it degenerates into "find all the images that don't suck… pulling out the crap until you get to the stuff that's just perfectly mediocre."
- **Pye Jirsa** (Lin & Jirsa): the finer grades are *unknowable at cull time* — "Trying to make that decision during the initial culling is not only time consuming, **it is impossible to make correctly**." His system leaves "only 1 choice: keep the image… or reject."
- **Victoria Bampton** (Lightroom Queen), the calibration-drift argument: "you could spend ages trying to figure out whether a photo deserves 2 stars or 3, and as your photography improves, your older 3 star photos might only count as 2 star photos now." A stored score silently rots as the standard moves; a binary decision made under a stated question does not.
- **Alex Cooke** (Fstoppers): "that combinatorial explosion is the enemy of speed", and the rule that makes a reject pass safe — "If a photo is not clearly bad during your reject pass, **it survives by default**."
- **Camera Bits' own documentation**: "Tagging is distinct from Color Class as it is **an on/off facility**"; a photo "is either tagged or untagged", on a single key. Colour class is the separate 8-state editorial axis.
- **Adobe's own docs** describe a multi-pass primitive: Library > **Refine Photos** re-baselines between rounds, turning "unflagged photos to be flagged as rejected, and picked photos to be unflagged". The tool assumes you will run the same binary decision again with a raised bar.

**Where stars are used well, they are semantic rather than ordinal.** Ming Thein: "3\* are kept because they're useful for stock or remembering a particular event; 4\* are good images and deliverable to clients; 5\* are portfolio grade." Each level answers *what is this for*, not *how good is it* — and note that "remembering a particular event" is a named tier that has nothing to do with quality. Bampton's ladder likewise defines each level by the work it earns (1 star = "no extra time spent", 4 stars "only added on a later pass through the 3 star photos"). And when Thein does need a ranking he builds it as a **promotion tournament** of binary rounds: "I'll go through all images and promote the better images to one star; then go through the one stars and promote to two; and so on… Round half-stars down to be disciplined." The grade is an *output* of repeated binary passes, never an input.

The most explicit published procedure I found is **Mike Davis**'s (twice POYi Picture Editor of the Year, ex-Nat Geo, ex-White House), and it is three passes with three different questions and a *pass/fail* rule rather than a score. Pass 1 is five objective criteria — "quality of light, color, moment value, composition and whether the distance from the subject is appropriate" — adjudicated by a two-strikes rule: "If any two of these aspects don't work, chances are the photo doesn't work. But if any one or two really, really works, then the whole photo probably works well." Pass 2 hands over to the eye: "Now move away from the objective and ask yourself if you feel something when you look at each of the photos you've moved forward. If the answer is yes, elevate the photos that elicit a response." Pass 3 is agglomeration: "Then put photos next to each other that create an even greater response and put those next to others that create yet more of an experience and you've created a story, a book, a gallery show." Note that pass 1 can be *rescued by excellence on one axis* — the criteria are not additive. Davis also names the anti-rule rule: "if you find yourself applying rules to pictures, stop that." His one-line thesis, on the B&H podcast: "Selecting photos is a different skill than making them."

**Documentary editors are split, and the sceptics are the interesting half.** Gabriel Rhodes (*Time*): "I don't categorize anything. I don't try and put it into baskets. I just want to really respond to the material… I do it through markers. I take tons of notes in the markers" — and the payoff of keeping notes rather than verdicts: "Over the course of the edit I would go back to those markers repeatedly and find moments that I had dismissed… once the edit started to unfold, it would re-contextualize those moments." Pax Wassermann (*Cartel Land*) names the shortlist trap outright: "**Selects become a burden at a certain point. You can become beholden to the selects when what you really need to do is just look at the footage.**" Zac Stuart-Pontier: "You don't know what's important yet." Todd Douglas Miller (*Apollo 11*) keeps no structure at all: "I just have a bin that says footage… I like to go through footage time and time and time again." Andy Grieve: "After I cut selects for an interview, you don't even use half of them. Then I go back to the transcript and use stuff you had no idea was going to be useful" — and his working rule, "I never tie knots I don't think I can untie." On the graded side, Chad Beck (*The Last Dance*) uses a visible two-tier bump — good lines to track two, "if there's something REALLY good… track three" — and Maya Mumma keeps an escape valve: "I always have a sequence called 'good things,' which I throw things into that I don't want to forget."

**Binary verdicts, but never bare ones.** Murch's own note-taking rule cuts against storing a label without a reason: "Avoid writing 'NG' (no good) without saying why it is no good. **Frequently, what is bad when you first see it may be exactly what you want two months later.**" And: "The worst thing is simply to write 'good' or 'no good' because that doesn't tell you anything much." A stored yes/no with no recorded reason cannot be re-examined when the question changes — which it will.

Bruce Gilden describes the same two-stage shape at burst level: "When I look at a contact sheet, I go in order from No. 1 to No. 36" and "I mark the ones I like and, unless something really jumps off the page at me, I go over them again to see which is the best one" (Magnum, *Theory & Practice*). Mark candidates on a linear sweep; compare only the marked set.

**Why not score, in four lines** (synthesis, following from the sources above):

1. A score pretends the comparison is global when it is local. On Hurn's contact sheets a frame is only ever better or worse *than the other frames of the same situation*. A 4 in one burst and a 4 in another are not the same thing.
2. Scores price each image as if it were sold separately. Halpern: "I'd much rather look at a series of pictures where the images are individually not as strong in a formal compositional sense, but that somehow feel like a paragraph spoken with a clear voice." No per-image number expresses that.
3. Marking is asymmetric in practice — you record hits, not misses. Jeffrey Ford, ACE (*Avengers: Infinity War*), on dailies notes: "It's not that important for me to write down what's bad. It's really only important to write down moments of performance that pop." Hurn's white pencil is the same instrument.
4. The decision that ships is a cut, not a rank. Murch renders the editor's inner monologue while scanning material as a string of binary verdicts: "No. No. No. No. No. No. No. No. No. No. Yes!" And Murch says his own famous percentages are a rhetorical device — in his *Art of the Cut* follow-up he calls them somewhat facetious, admitting he "had to make the numbers jump around" to make emotion come out on top. **The ordering is the tool; the numbers are packaging.**

Murch's Rule of Six is therefore a **lexicographic priority order, not a weighted sum** (percentages as quoted in the Berkeley i290 course notes): Emotion 51%, Story 23%, Rhythm 10%, Eye-trace 7%, Two-dimensional plane of the screen 5%, Three-dimensional space of action 4%. His conflict rule is not to average: "If you find you have to sacrifice certain of those six things to make a cut, sacrifice your way up, item by item, from the bottom." From the book: "Don't give up emotion before story. Don't give up story before rhythm, don't give up rhythm before eye-trace, don't give up eye-trace before planarity, and don't give up planarity before spatial continuity." Emotion "is the thing that you should try to preserve at all costs", because "what they finally remember is not the editing, not the camerawork, not the performances, not even the story — it's how they felt." Lindsay Utz, editing *American Factory* seventy years later, states the same operating rule: "I'm emotion first. That's how I base my decisions. Is the scene working emotionally?"

## 3. First pass: what disqualifies, and what never does

**Disqualifies outright.** Alex Cooke's pass-1 list is representative: "Misfires, frames where autofocus locked onto the background, blinks, test shots." Ming Thein's is the more interesting one, because he **splits it by what the display can actually support**. On the camera back: "Clearly out of focus / Incorrectly exposed / Compositional failures… / Clearly meaningless / no obvious subject" — but "**I'll leave duplicates or near-duplicates of good shots; you can't judge fine detail or critical focus off the back of a camera screen.**" Only at full screen does he add: "Not critically sharp / Didn't work… at a reasonable size / Compositionally weaker than the rest of the set." A pass only gets to ask questions its viewing conditions can answer. Jared Platt orders it attention-first, technique-second: pick what "catches my attention", then "zoom in to make sure it's sharp". Hurn adds a craft-specific disqualifier — a frame you cannot actually *see*: "you must be able to see the picture clearly."

**Never disqualifies, if the content matters.** The load-bearing case is Robert Capa's Omaha Beach frames, 6 June 1944 — but **the famous version of the story is false and should not be repeated.** A.D. Coleman's research finds "no plausible possibility of a darkroom accident exists" and that the negatives "show no sign of damage of any sort"; the blur is genuine motion blur. John G. Morris, LIFE's picture editor, recanted late in life (NYT *Lens*, 2016: "Mr. Morris now thinks that the negatives were not melted"). Counts conflict — Capa in *Slightly Out of Focus* says "Out of one hundred and six pictures in all, only eight, were salvaged"; Morris says "on the fourth roll there were eleven frames worth printing"; Coleman says ten. LIFE printed **five**, on 19 June 1944, and captioned them "slightly out of focus" — the flaw not hidden but recaptioned as evidence of the conditions of capture. The craft fact survives the myth: **shaky, grainy frames ran on the strength of being the only frames in existence of that morning.**

Morris also gives the cleanest statement of the priority order in journalism: "In journalism, one first looks for the meaning, the truth that's involved in the image – does this image show something important? Is it true or false? The composition, the form, which is more of an aesthetic question, comes second" (Magnum, *Theory & Practice*). And on the asymmetry of the job: "As with any picture editor, my job was to find the good images and not really to worry about the bad or non-existent ones." First-person versions of the same override: Mark Power kept a blurred one-second-exposure frame despite four listed defects — "But no matter; it was still a remarkable piece of luck." Michael Arnold, on Eve Arnold's Marilyn frame: "the whole composition came together, never to be repeated." Zack Arias keeps weak frames for structural function — "a visual segue that I would not have thought of."

**The counter-position, and its scope.** Ming Thein rejects "near misses on any front", because "compromises to one image in a sequence of ten, twenty… large/high resolution prints… will be very obvious and weaken the whole body of work". His domain is repeatable, controlled, print-destined work; Morris's rule governs unrepeatable ones. Thein supplies the meta-rule that reconciles them: "The order of priority for curation should actually defer to the main weakness of the photographer."

Jeffrey Ford, ACE, gives film's version: "Sometimes we're not looking for 'correct.' We're looking for aberrations in behavior that give nuance." The craft term for the deliberately kept, aesthetically null frame is the **record shot** — a frame "where the emphasis is on creating a record of the photographed object, not trying to portray the object in an artistic representation" (Photokonnexion's definition; also The Online Photographer, "Record Shots", which calls them accurate and informative even when drab). Record shots are archived on purpose. They are not failures of the cull; they are a category the cull is not entitled to judge.

Hurn institutionalises this. His **first** listed purpose for a contact sheet is not selection at all: "I shoot a lot of what I call 'dear diary' pictures. Those images have no foreseeable use and I never intend to enlarge them. But they act as simple visual records, personal reminders of people met and places visited. I will also shoot a frame of a street sign, to give me the location of other frames on the same roll." And: "One of the photographer's greatest pleasures is to look back at ten-year-old contact sheets. The images provide an open door and total recall to the pleasures (and pains) of the past."

So the professional answer to "does a technically weak, banal frame survive?" is: **it is not in the same competition.** It is a record, judged on whether it records something, and the photographer keeps a separate lane for it.

## 4. Grouping and storylines

### The group is the unit, and the group is a *situation*

Hurn describes how a shoot decomposes, and it is not by similarity: "When I am looking for pictures I instinctively sense situations that might contain good shots. I then latch on to the situation and start shooting. Sometimes that action will build up into a climax in about six to ten shots, and then drop away. At other times, the action will start to build up ... but then stop at nothing. So you have to forget that scene and latch on to another pregnant situation."

His Henley Regatta example is a four-frame arc: girl fingering a ring → fiancé holds the ring finger → he kisses it → still kissing, photographer moves to improve the geometry → "That's it. He drops the finger, the action is over." And the shape those groups take on a sheet: "frames build up to a crescendo where a gesture, expression, or arrangement of shapes signal that the image is captured — or the sequence abruptly ends because the event has collapsed." That is the real form of the near-duplicate rule. A burst is not N similar images; it is **one attempt at one picture**, with a peak somewhere in it — or with no peak at all, in which case it yields **zero** keepers, not one. Hurn is explicit that this is not spray-and-pray: shooting many frames "was not a matter of banging out 36 images in the hope that one will survive: it was an awareness of small incidents — a tension in the wrist, a straightening of a finger, a bunched fist".

### Picking the winner among near-duplicates

From the *Magnum Contact Sheets* record (Kristen Lubben, ed., Thames & Hudson; detail via Eye magazine's review "One image, many photos"):

Cartier-Bresson, Seville 1933 — many frames of boys around a damaged wall, two circled in white Chinagraph; the keeper is the one where "all the elements — architectural and human — merged". Elliott Erwitt, 1953 — 27 frames before the one with his wife and baby in "rapt communication". Harry Callahan's *Cape Cod* — a volleyball net on an empty beach, which looks like a grab shot — is 11 frames on the sheet; Hurn: "he kept shooting from very slightly different angles and positions; he was not content with first impressions."

**Where in the run the keeper sits is contested, and the evidence leans late.** Elliott Erwitt on his bulldog roll: the keeper is "the very last picture on the roll", and "It's a lot of pictures getting to the good one." Thein, reading *Magnum Contact Sheets*, sees the same: "the photography greats… work the scene with at least one, sometimes more, rolls of film before they get what they need. It's an incremental process." Katelyn James makes it a workflow rule and culls **backwards** — "the BEST images are closer to the END of that section", so she starts at the reception and ends at the portraits. Against that, Kevin Mullins argues the peak is often the *first* frame: "make one or two frames, then lower the camera", and "the strongest image is the first one you made." Reconciling them (synthesis): where the photographer is *working toward* something, quality climbs through the run; where the subject is reacting to being photographed, it decays. Both are real; the run's own shape decides which.

**Tie-breakers are less codified than the teaching folklore suggests.** The tidy checklist — peak of gesture, eye contact, clean background — appears in **no primary source found** in this research, and should be treated as folklore rather than craft. What named practitioners actually say is vaguer and more interesting. Tony Wodarck names discriminators from the top down: scene first, then "micro adjustments of the couple. How their mouths and eyes look. Their hands, how they're leaning in to each other." Bruce Gilden's verdict is two-axis: "It worked, form-wise and emotionally." Jared Platt uses Lightroom's Survey view six-up and waits passively — "wait until one of the images catches my attention… allow the images to choose themselves". Alec Soth: "It's impossible to explain why I chose the final frame… It just felt right." What the Magnum sheets show, and what I'd call the closest thing to a stated criterion, is **completion of a configuration** — the frame where the elements line up (Cartier-Bresson's "all the elements… merged"). Marking conventions differ per photographer (Cartier-Bresson circled in white, Erwitt in red, Philip Jones Griffiths used green/white/blue/yellow plus stickers): the *marks* are personal, the *practice* uniform.

**How many from one situation.** Platt's economics: "**The more you have of something, the less each one is worth**… If I provide 1 image from the 6 and 1 from the next 6… I would have a wow factor on every image." Wodarck sets a quota: "Try not to include more than 2 photos or so from each scene… Let them LOVE each photo, don't make them decide which is best." duChemin generalises past visual similarity to *content* similarity: "If you're hitting the same note twice with two images put a red X through one of them." And Thein gives the set-level acceptance test: "Looking at the final set… **at a glance, one should not be mistaken for another**."

### Coverage vs strength: the tension, stated flatly

Hurn's picture-essay method inverts the usual instinct. Decide how many pictures the end use needs (7 for a magazine layout, 40 for a show, 120 for a book), then "divide your topic or theme into that many picture-headings", annotate each with "overall/establishment picture", "medium distance/relationship picture", "close-up picture" — then shoot to fill the headings and tick them off: "when you get a photograph which fits one of the headings you tick it off and do not photograph that any more." The failure mode he names is exactly over-selecting the strong image:

> "Ask somebody who has been to a protest march or demonstration what they remember about the event and they might
> reply: 'There were about 6,000 people there, most of them very quiet, many of them were middle class and a lot of
> them were women with kids.' ... When you look at their contacts you see five people in unusual clothes and a punch-up
> that lasted all of three minutes during the three-and-a-half-hour march. The pictures do not relate to the
> photographer's memory of the event. **Too often, the photographer looks for the visually strong picture rather than
> covering what actually happens.**"

And the anti-clustering rule, from his club example — don't spend nine nights out of ten on the stripper in the corner because she is the easy picture: "You do not end up with 80 percent of one situation and 20 percent covering the other dozen or so equally important happenings."

Film has the same category. Tom Cross, ACE (*Whiplash*): "You're looking for whatever connective tissue might help you join two pieces of film together" — connective material is chosen for what it does *between* two other things, a criterion invisible if you evaluate shots one at a time. Murch gives it a physical marker on his scene-card wall: the "connective-tissue scene" gets the thinnest card, colour-coded, because "the colors mean different things to me about the emotional content of the scene".

Sarah Leen holds both sides at once in her Magnum *Theory & Practice* interview: "The best image should be published no matter what the platform is. That is the first consideration: what is the best photograph for the story" — but for a print story, "we are all about the storytelling and not the single image, though every image in the story should be able to stand on its own." Her grouping is by kind, not quality or time: "I call them buckets that I put photos in ... by sort of issues and types of photos", and the question she asks running down the sequence is "what is this photo doing in terms of telling the story and the next one and the next one" (thecameracafeshow.com). Kathy Moran, Nat Geo senior editor of 30 years, picks photographers for consistency over peaks: "what I'm really looking for is consistency," because "when I'm doing my edit and making the lay-out, I know that each image will build on the other images" (LensCulture).

On why the strongest images cannot simply be taken. Gregory Halpern: "it really weakens it when one or two images feel like they compromised the set because you needed to get this one good picture in." Mark Power, on editing his archive with Stuart Smith: "Stu encouraged me to bring in pictures that weren't necessarily my 'favourites', and of course he was right. You can't have a relentless sequence of 'big pictures'. There have to be moments of quiet as well" — and on his own failed solo attempt at the same material, "I was simply using all my big-hitting pictures." Martin Parr states the arithmetic: "there might only be three great pictures, but you couldn't have a book with three pictures in." **The dissent, which is real and should not be smoothed over.** MaryAnne Golon (TIME Director of Photography for 15+ years, later Washington Post): "I reject is this idea that narrative storytelling should have 'filler pictures' that move you from one place to the next," and "each individual image can stand alone, but in their combination, they tell a story. They have to be powerful enough to stand by themselves" (LensCulture). Golon's standard is conjunctive — every picture must pass both tests. The plausible reconciliation is unit count: Golon is describing 8–15-picture news essays; Power and Halpern are describing 100+-picture books. **Connective tissue earns its place only when the set is long enough to need rests.**

W. Eugene Smith, to *Editor and Publisher* in 1948, on how the shape is held during shooting: "I bear in mind that I have to have an opener and closer. Then I make a mental picture of how to fill in between these two." And nightly: "Each night, I would mark the pictures that I took, or record my thoughts, on thousands of white cards ... I would start roughing in a layout of what pictures I had, and note how they build and what was missing in relationships" (Magnum, *Theory & Practice*). The layout is maintained *during* the shoot, as a gap detector — the same instrument as Hurn's headings, forty years earlier.

## 5. Sequencing and the final cut

**The single most useful line against top-N selection**, from Teun van der Heijden, designer of *Black Passport* and *Interrogations*: "If you simply select the best 50 images in a series, it's not an interesting book, because the tension in the photos is always the same. Like in a film or a novel, you have to build up to the tension and then you need to release the tension" (insideimaging.com.au roundup). A top-N set is *flat in tension*, therefore shapeless — which is the mechanism underneath Power's "moments of quiet".

Halpern describes sequencing as growth by pairs, not sorting — a ladder he credits to Todd Hido: "How can I make a pair for that picture? Give it some company. It could be a picture that's similar in content, or feeling, or light. Or it could even be an opposite… And then what you need is a third. And once you have three, then maybe it's the beginning of a phrase."

Mark Power on why pairs cannot be predicted from the items: "pairing two pictures you might be sure would work together often don't; for whatever reason they might cancel each other out. On the other hand two pictures that might not individually be so remarkable can each lift the other to be much better… and suggest a sort of 'third thing' floating somewhere between the two." Charles Harbutt, watching designer **Yolanda Cuomo** lay out a book (TIME), describes the bottom-up assembly: "trying this, trying that, laughing at a joke, then snatching a perfect pair and moving it to the end of her long work table to join other pairs and eventually to be melded into a riff, a sequence and the book." Pairs → runs → sequence, never a global sort. Two concrete, implementable rules surfaced. Jason Eskenazi: "a dark frame followed by a light frame … on one page you put something on the left, and in the next you put something on the right. You want the viewer to keep looking around the frame to push the viewer through the book." And a stated ceiling on how long a run can be before it stops cohering — John Gossage, reported by Power (second-hand, no primary source found): "it was impossible to sequence more than six or seven pictures before you have to start all over again"; Power pushed it to "nine or ten, in 16 page sections, each with a beginning and an end." A 196-picture book is built as ~20 self-contained runs, not one 196-long chain. Alec Soth: "Pictures rub up against each other and let the imagination kind of ping off in different directions" (Photoworks, "Dancing with Ourselves") — the same essay locates photobook authorship in "the filtering, editing, sequencing, positioning, pacing, shaping, shifting, sculpting and interweaving", where filtering is one verb of nine.

Hurn's final structural check is done on the whole set at once and is about **pace, not quality**: tack the images on a wall and "see if there is enough visual variety within the set. ... You might have too many images taken from the same distance. So you choose the best image from that group and re-shoot the weaker ones as close-ups or long-shots or in any manner which imparts a sense of pace to the final set." Note the shape: a *set-level* redundancy pass that groups by shot distance, keeps one per group, and treats the resulting holes as work to be done rather than an acceptable result. Frank's method was the same at book scale — ~1,000 prints on the floor and walls, a year of arranging. The wall exists because pairs and runs cannot be judged in a list.

"Kill your darlings" is Arthur Quiller-Couch, 1914 (*On the Art of Writing*, 1916), not Faulkner or King: "Whenever you feel an impulse to perpetrate a piece of exceptionally fine writing, obey it — whole-heartedly — and delete it before sending your manuscript to press." The original is narrower and more useful than the slogan: cut the piece that is *conspicuously excellent relative to the work around it*. Steve Audette gives documentary's operational test: "you have to murder your darlings", and "If the scene you're considering cutting does not change the straight line of our protagonist, then it can be cut… Often in a documentary there's a very good story, an extremely interesting story but it's off the plumb line of our protagonist tale. So cut it out." Ken Schneider names the effort-bias version: "No viewer cares if the filmmakers braved lava beasts to peer over the edge of the caldera and get the shot of the smoldering pit, if it doesn't advance the story." Elliot Graham: "The only thing that's sacred is the film in its entirety. Does it work?" And Scorsese, on calibration: "No film is ever as good as the dailies or as bad as the first cut."

Dylan Highsmith puts the trade in five words (*Art of the Cut*): "Sacrifice a good edit for better storytelling." Stephen Mirrione, ACE (*The Revenant*), gives the positive form: "It's about finding a moment to respond to, deciding 'This moment is the anchor of this scene'" — one item per scene is load-bearing and the rest is arranged around it.

The distinction every source circles: **a good photograph and a photograph that belongs in this edit are different objects.** Halpern takes the weaker-but-coherent run; Hurn cuts the punch-up that misrepresents the march; Murch sacrifices spatial continuity to protect emotion. Nobody resolves this by adding the two scores together.

## 6. Taste vs process

Murch, in *In the Blink of an Eye*: "You could sit in one room with a pile of dailies and another editor could sit in the next room with exactly the same footage and both of you would make different films out of the same material." (Corroborated across two independent sets of reading notes; not verified against a print copy.) He also describes the act as finding rather than building: "Editing … is not so much a putting together as it is a discovery of a path."

But the divergence is not total. John Morris, on editing alongside Cartier-Bresson: "often we would edit the same contacts and I would almost always end up picking the same negatives that he did." Two trained eyes on one take converge more often than not. Where they *don't*, Mike Davis names the mechanism from running contest panels: "each judge votes for a certain realm of pictures. One won't see pictures that are outside of that realm and therefore won't vote for any photo that doesn't fall in that realm, on the first pass ... Sometimes, if another judge votes for a given image that moves forward to a second round, a judge who didn't vote for that photo before will clue into what's working in the photo and will vote for it." His summary: "It's as if we all have filters over our eyes that only allow us to see certain types of pictures." The fix is procedural — mixed panels plus multi-round promotion, so a frame rescued by one judge gets re-seen by the rest. That is exactly what World Press Photo's round structure implements. Davis is also the best source on why the maker cannot edit himself, and the reasons are specific: "Photographers tend to pass over their photos that were easy to make. And they overvalue photos that were very difficult to make or photos that gave them a great experience or included great smells or sounds - **things that you can't tell from looking at a picture**. In the end, the photo is not about you, it's a freestanding entity that exists independent of the experience or the person who made it." Zack Arias, plainly: "I, for one, can not edit and sequence my own work. **I'm too close to it.**" duChemin's version of the outside-eye method is procedural enough to copy: show someone the set, have them sort it into two piles — "the stuff that moved them and the stuff that was just ok" — and then "ask them to explain WHY". Disagreement is diagnostic, not corrective: "You've just uncovered your own preference." His discipline for the final pass is "Edit them down until it hurts a little. **Editing should hurt a little**", and his precondition is a target number, fixed before looking: "It is infinitely easier for me to edit 20,000 images down if I know I need 12." Thein names the two-sided failure — "overcritical (bin all) or sentimental (bin none) – neither approach is good" — and the frame for the whole business: "**you are judged on what you show, not what you shoot.**"

Teachable and universal, on this evidence: multiple passes asking **different questions**, not one pass asking one; a cooling-off gap between making and judging; second eyes; group-then-choose rather than rank-then-take-top-N; judging the set spatially and whole before locking; and coverage discipline (headings, shot-distance variety, pace). On second eyes, Hurn: "I like to show the contacts to a photographer I respect. This person is unaware of my feelings, can cut through the memories and fantasies, and will only see what is actually there, in the image itself." Halpern: "I can't do it alone. I need other eyes to help me figure out what makes sense," and on receiving an edit, "Just take notes ... don't talk. Just listen."

Not transferable: which frame in the burst is the peak, what the story is, and what counts as emotion. Hurn concedes even the heading list "will depend to a considerable extent on the personality of the photographer." ADE says the same of documentary: the editor "is also a writer", and "it is in fact in the edit that the vision or story is discovered and/or written."

Formal juries handle taste by *structuring* it rather than removing it. World Press Photo runs several rounds over six weeks: six regional juries of five make a first selection, then a global jury (the six regional chairs plus a global chair) picks winners; a non-voting secretary enforces procedure only, and early rounds are anonymous. Elimination in rounds, by different eyes, with context revealed progressively — never a single scored ranking. Its stated criteria are visual quality ("creativity, technical skill and visual style, and the edit of the story"), story ("its relevance or significance, and the way in which the photographer has chosen to approach the story") and representation — with juries given "freedom to determine and define what makes an entry worthy". **There is no published rubric weighting document value against execution: the trade-off is deliberately left uncodified and delegated to humans, case by case.**

## 7. The edit's thesis, and the once-only moment

### Does the story come first?

In documentary, overwhelmingly **later**. Joe Bini (long-time Werner Herzog editor), in the ADE guide: "With a narrative film, you start with the script. In a documentary, it might be **3 months before you have the first idea of what the story actually is**." ADE's own framing: "In most documentaries and documentary series, there is no script. Experienced directors, producers, and editors know that it is in fact in the edit that the vision or story is discovered and/or written."

Three more, all named. Thelma Schoonmaker: "Usually, in a documentary, you are creating a script, in a way. You're given tons of footage." Jonathan Oppenheim (*Paris Is Burning*) — "we find out what the thing is really about and try to make that the center of gravity, the organizing principle of the story"; Tom Haneke — "We're not following a script… You're often writing the show as you cut it", and "I begin to discover what the film is actually about"; Doug Pray (*The Defiant Ones*) — "It all sort of crystallizes at the end and not at the beginning… it's sort of a reverse writing process." The split is explicit *inside* the profession: in the same IDA roundtable Larry Silk tells Haneke "I think you're more conceptually organized earlier in the process than I am", and Haneke replies "I don't think it's necessarily a better approach."

Robert Frank went the other way deliberately. His 1955 Guggenheim application proposes only "observation and record of what one naturalized American finds to see in the United States". What he was refusing is explicit: "I didn't want to produce what everybody else was producing… not to make a Life story. Those goddamned stories with a beginning and an end." SFMOMA's exhibition included "three collages (made from more than 115 original rough work prints) assembled under Frank's supervision… revealing his intended themes as well as his first rounds of images selection" — material evidence that the themes were *found in the print pile*. Seventy years on, scholars still disagree about what the book's organising themes are (Sarah Greenough reads four flag-opened sections; Philip Monk objects that the themes are "spread evenly throughout the different sections"). The thesis of the most-edited photobook in history is still contested — which is itself a finding.

The counter-practice is National Geographic, which fixes a thesis first and then *budgets for being wrong*. David Griffin, then Director of Photography: an accepted "story proposal" becomes a "story coverage plan, including estimated budget"; the story is shot in roughly two trips "so that half way through the coverage the story team can re-gather, review the photographs to-date, and make any necessary course corrections" at an **Interim Projection**, followed by a **Final Projection** where "the photographer typically comes in to headquarters and works with the photo editor to hone the completed coverage" (aphotoeditor.com). A scheduled, funded checkpoint whose explicit purpose is to let the material overrule the plan. Hurn sits between them: his picture-headings are written after one reconnaissance visit, before serious shooting, and exist so the photographer can see what is missing and "know when you have completed the story" — but "it does not mean that you must rigidly adhere to every picture-heading if the reality is different from your preconceptions."

The honest description is neither pure emergence nor pure imposition: **a provisional thesis that is cheap to state, used mainly to detect gaps, and revised against the material.** (Synthesis, but it is what both sources describe.) Murch supplies the mechanism: "You are actually doing creative work, and you may find what you really want rather than what you thought you wanted."

### The once-only moment

There is no famous craft doctrine that says "keep the ugly frame of the pregnancy test", because professional edits are of assignments, not lives. Three threads converge on it, and they are the strongest answer available:

1. **The record shot** (§3) — an explicitly separate category, kept for what it documents, judged on accuracy and informativeness rather than beauty.
2. **Hurn's "dear diary" frames** (§3) — pictures with "no foreseeable use", kept as "simple visual records, personal reminders", including deliberately shooting a street sign purely as a location marker. These never enter the aesthetic edit at all.
3. **Bill Jay on memory-joggers**, about his own portraits of photographers, made for no publication: "Certainly when I am looking through the contacts I am more interested in the images which most strongly conjure up the individual's words, the tone of voice, the personality, **than I am concerned with the best picture. It is the most potent memory-joggers which are the most valuable.**"

The whole essay method is defended on the same ground — Hurn: "The aim is to take images which become your memory of the event," and "My best pictures are the ones that say: 'That's what I remember the place to be like.'" Capa's D-Day frames are the extreme case under deadline: they survived because nothing else covered the beat, and the state of the negative was simply not the question being asked. My synthesis of the rule the craft actually follows: **an image's value is relational, not intrinsic — it depends on what else exists.** The frame that is the only evidence of an event is being judged on a different axis from the frame that is one of forty attempts at a good picture, and any process that runs them through the same comparison will always delete the first one.

## Mapping hints

Not a design — only which craft pass corresponds to which pipeline concept.

1. **Junk cull** = photographers' pass 1 (Hurn's white mark, the P/X flag). Binary, fast, per-item; asks only "is there a picture here", never "how good". Cooke's default: anything not *clearly* bad survives.
2. **A pass may only ask what its viewing conditions can answer** (Thein's camera-back list vs his full-screen list). Near-duplicate collapse cannot be done at the same fidelity as blink detection.
3. **Near-duplicate collapse** = "work the scene, keep the peak". The group is a *situation*, not a similarity cluster, and may legitimately yield **zero** keepers. The named tie-breakers are folklore; what practitioners actually cite is completion of a configuration. Where the keeper sits in the run is contested (Erwitt/James: late; Mullins: first).
4. **Selects are not aggressive.** Eisenhardt: documentary selects keep 25–50%. The big cuts happen later, at structure, not at the item filter.
5. **Story grouping** = stringouts + Hurn's picture-headings + Leen's "buckets". Coverage is first-class: headings get ticked off, and an over-covered heading stops competing (Platt: "the more you have of something, the less each one is worth").
6. **Repeat the binary, don't refine a score.** Thein's promotion tournament makes the grade an output of repeated binary rounds; his intersection rule keeps only what two independent passes both chose.
7. **Never store a bare verdict.** Murch: "Avoid writing 'NG' … what is bad when you first see it may be exactly what you want two months later." Store the reason, so the decision survives a change of question.
8. **Beware the shortlist trap.** Wassermann: "You can become beholden to the selects when what you really need to do is just look at the footage." Rhodes reopens dismissed material once the edit re-contextualises it.
9. **Holistic final review** = the wall / floor / contact-sheet pass — set-level redundancy, pacing, adjacent pairs, opener and closer. Cannot be done on a ranked list. Van der Heijden: a top-N set is flat in tension, therefore shapeless.
10. **Priority under conflict** = Murch's lexicographic order, emotion first — sacrifice from the bottom up, never average criteria into one number.
11. **Once-only moments** = a separate lane (record shots / Hurn's "dear diary" frames), exempted from the aesthetic comparison rather than given a bonus inside it. Value is relational: the question is "is this the only one", not "is this good".
12. **Fix the target number before looking** (duChemin: "infinitely easier … if I know I need 12"), and treat the thesis as provisional, cheap and revisable — used to detect what is *missing*, not to rank what is present.
